Chapter 01

Signals, and what discrete time changes

A signal is just information written as a function of time. Sample it and two things change that catch people out every year: frequency stops being unbounded, and a sinusoid stops being automatically periodic. This chapter settles the classification of signals, the energy-versus-power test, and the one-line periodicity test the paper likes to open with.

1.1 The classes that matter

Sort a signal first. The method that works on one class often fails on another, and the exam asks you to name the class before anything else.

  • Continuous-time or discrete-time — is the time axis a line or a list of whole numbers? Write x(t) for one, x(n) for the other.
  • Deterministic or random — can you write a formula for every future value, or only a probability?
  • Periodic or aperiodic — does it repeat exactly after some N?
  • Energy or power — does the total squared value settle to a finite number, or does only the average settle?
  • Even or odd — mirror image about n = 0, or anti-mirror.

1.2 Energy signals and power signals

In discrete time, adding up replaces integrating. Two sums, and a signal can only be one of the two kinds.

E = sum_{n=-inf}^{inf} |x(n)|^2 P = lim (1/(2N+1)) sum_{n=-N}^{N} |x(n)|^2 periodic signal: P = (1/N) sum_{n=0}^{N-1} |x(n)|^2
The two-way rule

If E is finite then P = 0, and the signal is an energy signal. If E is infinite but P is finite and non-zero, it is a power signal. Anything periodic is a power signal, because it never dies away. A decaying exponential is an energy signal.

1.3 Frequency: continuous against discrete

For an analog sinusoid cos(2*pi*F*t) the frequency F can be anything at all, and every different F is a different signal. Once the time axis is a list of integers none of that survives. Write the digital frequency as f = F/Fs, or in radians per sample as w = 2*pi*f.

Fastest oscillationNo fastest — F can keep risingAt ω = π (f = ½), one sign change per sample

1.4 The periodicity test

The test, in one line

cos(w0*n + phi) is periodic only if w0/(2*pi) is a ratio of two whole numbers. Write f = k/N in lowest terms; then N is the fundamental period. For a sum of sinusoids, take the LCM of the individual periods.

The trap

cos(3n) is not periodic. Here f = 3/(2π), and π is irrational, so no whole number of samples ever brings the wave back to where it started. The continuous signal cos(3t) is perfectly periodic — sampling destroyed it.

Worked example

Say whether each is periodic, give the period, and say whether it is an energy or a power signal.

(a) x(n) = cos(pi*n/8 + 0.2) f = (pi/8)/(2*pi) = 1/16 ratio of whole numbers --> periodic N = 16 (the phase 0.2 shifts it, it cannot change N) P = average of cos^2 over one period = 0.5 , E = infinite --> power signal, average power 1/2 (b) x(n) = cos(3n) f = 3/(2*pi) irrational --> NOT periodic bounded but never decays --> still a power signal (c) x(n) = (1/2)^n u(n) E = sum_{n=0}^{inf} (1/4)^n = 1/(1 - 1/4) = 4/3 finite --> energy signal, E = 4/3 , P = 0 (d) x(n) = cos(pi*n/3) + sin(pi*n/4) first term : f = 1/6 --> N1 = 6 second term: f = 1/8 --> N2 = 8 N = LCM(6, 8) = 24 --> periodic, N = 24

Checked numerically: x(n) in (d) repeats at a lag of 24 and at no smaller lag — a lag of 12 does not reproduce it.

Exam tip

Periodicity and energy-versus-power come as one four-mark opener. Answer in this order every time: write f, say rational or not, give N or say “not periodic”, then one sentence on energy versus power. Four lines, four marks.

Chapter 01 at a glance
  • Digital frequency f = F/Fs; only −½ ≤ f ≤ ½ is distinct, and ω and ω + 2πk are the same signal.
  • A discrete sinusoid is periodic only if f is a ratio of whole numbers; write f = k/N in lowest terms and N is the period.
  • For a sum of sinusoids the period is the LCM of the individual periods.
  • Energy signal ⇒ E finite and P = 0. Power signal ⇒ E infinite and P finite. Every periodic signal is a power signal.
  • The fastest discrete oscillation is at ω = π: one sign change per sample. There is nothing faster.

Chapter 02

Sampling, aliasing and quantization

Sampling is the one step where information can be lost for good. This chapter settles how fast you must sample, exactly which frequency a too-fast signal comes back as, and what the extra error from rounding each sample costs you in decibels.

2.1 The sampling relation

Take a reading every Ts seconds. The analog signal becomes a list of numbers, and the analog frequency F becomes the digital frequency f.

x(n) = x_a(n*Ts) Ts = 1/Fs f = F / Fs w = 2*pi*f = Omega*Ts -1/2 <= f <= 1/2 <--> -Fs/2 <= F <= Fs/2

2.2 The sampling theorem

The theorem

A signal band-limited to Fmax is recovered exactly from its samples provided Fs > 2*Fmax. The number 2*Fmax is the Nyquist rate. Half the sampling rate, Fs/2, is the folding frequency — the highest frequency the sampled signal can represent.

Two words that get swapped

The Nyquist rate is a property of the signal: twice its highest frequency. The folding frequency is a property of the sampler: half its rate. Saying “Nyquist frequency” for either one is how marks go missing.

2.3 Aliasing: which frequency you actually get

Every analog frequency in the family F0 + k*Fs lands on exactly the same set of samples. If a component sits above the folding frequency it does not vanish — it comes back wearing a different, lower frequency, and nothing downstream can tell the difference.

aliases of F0 : F0 + k*Fs for every integer k to find the apparent frequency: f = F/Fs , then add or subtract whole numbers until -1/2 <= f <= 1/2 apparent F = |f| * Fs
Worked example

The signal x_a(t) = 3cos(2*pi*100*t) + 2cos(2*pi*300*t) + cos(2*pi*600*t) is sampled at Fs = 500 Hz. What comes out?

folding frequency = Fs/2 = 250 Hz 100 Hz : f = 100/500 = 0.20 already inside +/-1/2 --> 100 Hz 300 Hz : f = 300/500 = 0.60 0.60 - 1 = -0.40 --> 200 Hz 600 Hz : f = 600/500 = 1.20 1.20 - 1 = +0.20 --> 100 Hz

So the sampled signal contains only 100 Hz and 200 Hz. Worse, the 600 Hz component has landed on top of the genuine 100 Hz one and added to it. That damage cannot be undone afterwards.

Fs needed for no aliasing = 2 * Fmax = 2 * 600 = 1200 Hz

Verified by sampling all three cosines: cos(2*pi*600*t) and cos(2*pi*100*t) agree at every sample instant, and so do cos(2*pi*300*t) and cos(2*pi*200*t).

Exam tip

Always answer an aliasing question in Hz and say which components collide. “600 Hz appears as 100 Hz” is half a mark; “600 Hz appears as 100 Hz and is therefore inseparable from the true 100 Hz component” is the full mark.

2.4 Quantization and coding

Sampling fixed the time axis. Quantization rounds each sample to one of L allowed levels, and coding writes that level in b bits, so L = 2^b. This step is where the error comes from — and unlike aliasing it is always present.

step size D = (x_max - x_min) / (L - 1) error bound |e_q(n)| <= D/2 SQNR (dB) = 1.76 + 6.02*b
The number to remember

Each extra bit buys about 6 dB of signal-to-quantization-noise ratio. At b = 8 that is 49.9 dB; at b = 16 it is 98.1 dB. If a question says “how many bits for 60 dB”, solve 60 = 1.76 + 6.02*b and round up — b = 10.

Chapter 02 at a glance
  • Sample faster than twice the highest frequency: Fs > 2*Fmax. The Nyquist rate is 2*Fmax; the folding frequency is Fs/2.
  • Every frequency in the family F0 + k*Fs gives identical samples — that is aliasing.
  • To find the apparent frequency: form f = F/Fs, slide it into −½ ≤ f ≤ ½ by whole numbers, multiply back by Fs.
  • Aliasing is not recoverable: two components can land on the same digital frequency and add.
  • Quantization step D = (x_max - x_min)/(L-1), error at most D/2, and SQNR = 1.76 + 6.02b dB — about 6 dB per bit.

Chapter 03

Discrete-time signals: writing them, moving them

Short chapter, but it is the grammar for everything after it. Four ways to write one signal, the handful of elementary signals every answer is built from, and the one operation ordering — fold against shift — that wrecks more convolution answers than any arithmetic slip.

3.1 Four ways to write the same signal

  1. Functional — x(n) = (0.5)^n for n ≥ 0, 0 otherwise.
  2. Tabular — two rows, n on top and x(n) underneath.
  3. Sequence — x(n) = {1, 2, 3, 4} with an arrow marking n = 0.
  4. Graphical — the stem plot.
The arrow is not decoration

In sequence notation the arrow says where n = 0 sits. Without it {1, 2, 3} is three different signals. Track the arrow through every shift, fold and convolution — losing it is the single most expensive habit in this subject.

3.2 The elementary signals

unit impulse d(n) = 1 at n = 0, else 0 unit step u(n) = 1 for n >= 0, else 0 unit ramp r(n) = n for n >= 0, else 0 exponential x(n) = a^n u(n) links: d(n) = u(n) - u(n-1) u(n) = sum_{k=-inf}^{n} d(k)
The three sifting facts

x(n)d(n-k) = x(k)d(n-k)  ·  sum x(n)d(n-k) = x(k)  ·  x(n) = sum_k x(k)d(n-k). The third one is the whole reason convolution exists: it writes any signal as a row of weighted impulses.

3.3 Operations on the index

Three things can be done to n: shift it, fold it, scale it. Shifting by k gives x(n-k) — a delay to the right for positive k. Folding gives x(-n) — a mirror about n = 0. Scaling by an integer M gives x(Mn), which throws samples away, and x(n/M), which needs samples invented in between.

Fold and shift do not commute

x(-n-k) is not x(-n+k). Always rewrite the argument in the form x(-(n + k)) or x(-(n - k)) first, then fold, then shift by whatever is left inside the bracket. Doing it by eye gets the answer reflected to the wrong side of the origin.

Worked example

Take x(n) = {1, 2, 3, 4} for n = 0, 1, 2, 3. Find y(n) = x(-n-2).

Step 1 -- rewrite the argument: x(-n-2) = x( -(n + 2) ) Step 2 -- fold: v(n) = x(-n) = {4, 3, 2, 1} at n = -3 .. 0 Step 3 -- ADVANCE by 2: y(n) = v(n + 2) = {4, 3, 2, 1} at n = -5 .. -2

Check straight from the definition: y(n) is non-zero when 0 <= -n-2 <= 3, that is -5 <= n <= -2. And y(-2) = x(0) = 1, y(-5) = x(3) = 4. Agrees.

the common wrong answer -- folding then DELAYING by 2: x(-n+2) = {4, 3, 2, 1} at n = -1 .. 2 <-- shifted 4 places wrong

Same four numbers, wrong place on the axis, no marks.

3.4 Even and odd parts

Any signal splits into one mirror-image part and one anti-mirror part, and the split is unique.

x_e(n) = ( x(n) + x(-n) ) / 2 x_e(-n) = x_e(n) x_o(n) = ( x(n) - x(-n) ) / 2 x_o(-n) = -x_o(n) x(n) = x_e(n) + x_o(n) x_o(0) = 0 always
Exam tip

The free check is x_o(0) = 0. If your odd part has a non-zero value at the origin, you folded wrongly. Takes two seconds.

Chapter 03 at a glance
  • Four notations for one signal — functional, tabular, sequence, graphical. In sequence form the arrow marks n = 0 and must be carried everywhere.
  • d(n) = u(n) - u(n-1), and x(n) = sum_k x(k)d(n-k) — any signal is a row of weighted impulses. That identity is where convolution comes from.
  • x(n-k) delays to the right for positive k; x(-n) mirrors about the origin.
  • Fold and shift do not commute: rewrite as x(-(n+k)) first, then fold, then shift by what is left in the bracket.
  • Even part (x(n)+x(-n))/2, odd part (x(n)-x(-n))/2, and the odd part is always zero at n = 0.

Chapter 04

LTI systems and convolution

Five yes-or-no questions classify any discrete-time system, and if the answers are linear and time-invariant then one signal — the impulse response h(n) — describes the system completely. This chapter settles the five tests, the two ways of computing a convolution by hand, and the checks that catch a wrong answer before you hand it in.

4.1 The five classification tests

PropertyThe testWhat fails it
Static (memoryless)y(n) depends only on x(n) at the same nAny x(n-1), any sum over past values
Time-invariantDelay the input by k; the output must be the old output delayed by kA coefficient that contains n, or x(2n), or x(-n)
LinearSuperposition: T[a*x1 + b*x2] = a*T[x1] + b*T[x2]Squares, products of samples, or an added constant
Causaly(n) uses no future inputAny x(n+1) term
BIBO stableEvery bounded input gives a bounded outputFor an LTI system: sum |h(n)| diverging
For an LTI system only

Causal ⇔ h(n) = 0 for n < 0.   Stable ⇔ sum |h(n)| < infinity. Both tests read straight off h(n) — no trial inputs needed. An FIR system is always stable, because a finite sum of finite numbers cannot diverge.

4.2 Where convolution comes from

Break the input into impulses, let the system answer each one, add the answers. Linearity allows the adding, time invariance says every impulse gets the same answer just shifted, and that is the whole derivation.

y(n) = sum_{k=-inf}^{inf} x(k) h(n-k) = sum_k h(k) x(n-k)

4.3 Computing one by hand

Two methods. The definition is fold, shift, multiply, add — once per output sample. The faster one for short sequences is the multiplication layout: convolution is polynomial multiplication, so line the numbers up and add the columns.

Three rules that cost nothing

Length of the result = n1 + n2 − 1. Start index of the result = start of x + start of h. Sum check: sum y = (sum x)(sum h).

Worked example

Two finite sequences. x(n) = {1, 2, 3} from n = 0 and h(n) = {1, -1, 2} from n = 0.

length = 3 + 3 - 1 = 5 , starting at n = 0 + 0 = 0 y(0) = 1*1 = 1 y(1) = 1*(-1) + 2*1 = 1 y(2) = 1*2 + 2*(-1) + 3*1 = 3 y(3) = 2*2 + 3*(-1) = 1 y(4) = 3*2 = 6 y(n) = { 1, 1, 3, 1, 6 }

Sum check: sum y = 1+1+3+1+6 = 12, and (sum x)(sum h) = 6 × 2 = 12. Agrees.

Now the infinite case, which is the one the paper sets. Take x(n) = (1/3)^n u(n) and h(n) = (1/2)^n u(n).

y(n) = sum_{k=0}^{n} (1/3)^k (1/2)^(n-k) = (1/2)^n sum_{k=0}^{n} (2/3)^k = (1/2)^n * [ 1 - (2/3)^(n+1) ] / (1 - 2/3) = 3(1/2)^n [ 1 - (2/3)^(n+1) ] = 3(1/2)^n - 2(1/3)^n for n >= 0

First samples from the formula: 1, 0.8333, 0.5278, 0.3009 — identical to a direct numerical convolution. Note the shape of the answer: one decaying term per pole, which is exactly what the z-transform would have given.

4.4 Properties, and reading h(n)

  • Commutative x ∗ h = h ∗ x — you may fold whichever sequence is easier.
  • Associative — two systems in cascade have h = h1 ∗ h2.
  • Distributive — two systems in parallel have h = h1 + h2.
  • Step response = the running sum of h(n). Useful backwards too: h(n) = s(n) - s(n-1).
  • Two standard results worth knowing cold: u ∗ u = (n+1)u(n), and a^n u ∗ b^n u = (a^(n+1) - b^(n+1))/(a - b) for a ≠ b.
Exam tip

If a question hands you a step response and asks for the system, difference it: h(n) = s(n) - s(n-1). In the continuous version of the same question, differentiate. That single move is worth seven marks and it turned up in the 2025 paper.

Chapter 04 at a glance
  • For an LTI system, h(n) is everything: causal ⇔ h(n) = 0 for n < 0, stable ⇔ sum |h(n)| is finite. FIR is always stable.
  • y(n) = sum_k x(k)h(n-k) — break the input into impulses, add the answers.
  • Result length = n1 + n2 − 1, starting at (start of x + start of h).
  • Free check every time: sum y = (sum x)(sum h).
  • Cascade multiplies the responses (h1 ∗ h2); parallel adds them (h1 + h2).
  • Step response is the running sum of h; so h(n) = s(n) - s(n-1) recovers the system from its step response.

Chapter 05

Difference equations and the total response

A difference equation is the system written as arithmetic on past samples. One of these is solved in every single paper. This chapter settles the two decompositions of the answer, how to get the impulse response straight from the coefficients, and how to invert a system.

5.1 The equation and the blocks it stands for

Every discrete-time system is wired from five parts: an adder, a constant multiplier, a signal multiplier, a unit delay z-1, and a unit advance. The wiring diagram and the equation are the same statement.

y(n) = - sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k y(n-k) + sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k x(n-k) N = 0 -> no feedback -> FIR, h(n) has finite length N > 0 -> feedback -> IIR, h(n) goes on for ever

5.2 Solving by recursion

Set the initial conditions, then turn the handle: y(0), then y(1), then y(2). It always works and it never lies. What it does not give you is a closed formula — so use it to check a formula, not to replace one.

5.3 Homogeneous plus particular

  1. Homogeneous — set the right-hand side to zero, try y(n) = C*lambda^n, and solve the characteristic equation lambda^N + a1*lambda^(N-1) + ... + aN = 0.
  2. Particular — guess the same shape as the input and fit the constant.
  3. Add them, then fit the arbitrary constants using y(0), y(1), ... computed from the difference equation itself.
Input x(n)Try for y_p(n)

Roots to solution forms

Distinct real roots → sum Ci*lambda_i^n. A complex pair r*e^(+/-j*theta) → r^n [C1*cos(theta*n) + C2*sin(theta*n)]. A root repeated m times → r^n [C0 + C1*n + ... + C(m-1)*n^(m-1)].

5.4 The two decompositions

They are not the same cut

Zero-input has the natural form, but the zero-state response also contains a transient term built from the characteristic roots. So natural ≠ zero-input in general. If the question names one of the four words, give exactly that one.

Worked example

y(n) - 0.5*y(n-1) = x(n), with x(n) = (1/4)^n u(n) and the system initially relaxed. Find the complete response.

Homogeneous. lambda^n - 0.5*lambda^(n-1) = 0 -> lambda = 0.5 y_h(n) = C (0.5)^n Particular. input is (1/4)^n , so try y_p(n) = K(1/4)^n K(1/4)^n - 0.5*K(1/4)^(n-1) = (1/4)^n divide by (1/4)^n : K - 0.5*K*4 = 1 K - 2K = 1 -> K = -1 y_p(n) = -(1/4)^n Complete. y(n) = C(0.5)^n - (0.25)^n Initial condition. relaxed means y(-1) = 0, so from the equation at n = 0: y(0) = 0.5*y(-1) + x(0) = 0 + 1 = 1 y(0) = C - 1 = 1 -> C = 2
y(n) = 2(0.5)^n - (0.25)^n , n >= 0

Check against the raw recursion y(n) = 0.5*y(n-1) + x(n):

recursion : 1 , 0.75 , 0.4375 , 0.234375 , 0.121094 , ... formula : 1 , 0.75 , 0.4375 , 0.234375 , 0.121094 , ... identical

One decaying term from the system pole at 0.5, one from the input pole at 0.25 — that is always the shape.

5.5 Impulse response straight from the equation

The two-line recipe

Solve the homogeneous equation for h0(n) with the single condition h0(0) = 1 and all earlier values zero. Then combine the numerator coefficients: h(n) = b0*h0(n) + b1*h0(n-1) + ... + bM*h0(n-M).

5.6 The inverse system

The inverse undoes the system: cascade the two and you get back exactly what you put in, so h(n) ∗ h_inv(n) = d(n). To build it, swap the roles of x and y in the difference equation — the numerator becomes the denominator.

system : y(n) - a*y(n-1) = x(n) H(z) = 1/(1 - a z^-1) inverse: y(n) = x(n) - a*x(n-1) H_inv(z) = 1 - a z^-1 an IIR system inverts to an FIR one, and the other way round
The follow-up question

Inverting swaps poles and zeros. So a zero of H(z) outside the unit circle becomes a pole of the inverse outside the unit circle, and the inverse is then unstable. The examiner asks “describe its behaviour” precisely to see whether you say that.

Chapter 05 at a glance
  • No feedback → FIR and always stable; feedback → IIR and the roots decide stability.
  • Total = natural + forced = zero-input + zero-state — two different cuts of the same answer, and zero-state still contains a transient.
  • Homogeneous: solve lambda^N + a1*lambda^(N-1) + ... = 0; particular: guess the shape of the input; then fit the constants from y(0), y(1) computed by the equation.
  • Impulse response: homogeneous solution with h0(0) = 1, then h(n) = b0*h0(n) + b1*h0(n-1) + ....
  • The inverse system swaps x and y — and swaps poles with zeros, so an IIR system inverts to an FIR one.
  • Always verify a closed-form answer by running the recursion for three samples. It takes thirty seconds.

Chapter 06

The z-transform and the ROC

The z-transform turns convolution into multiplication and difference equations into algebra. But the algebra alone is only half an answer: the same X(z) stands for several different signals, and the region of convergence is what picks between them. This chapter settles the pairs, the properties, and the ROC rules.

6.1 The definition

X(z) = sum_{n=-inf}^{inf} x(n) z^-n ROC = the set of z for which that sum converges

Each sample becomes one term. The transform is a power series in z^-1, and reading the coefficients off a series is itself a way of inverting it.

6.2 The pairs that do almost everything

Rows four and five are the whole point

The same X(z) with two different ROCs gives two completely different signals — one right-sided, one left-sided, and the left-sided one carries a minus sign. An X(z) written without its ROC has not been answered.

6.3 The properties you actually use

Why the delay property is the workhorse

It turns y(n) - a*y(n-1) = x(n) into Y(z) - a*z^-1*Y(z) = X(z) in one line, and then H(z) = Y(z)/X(z) falls out by division. Almost every z-transform question in this paper starts with that single move.

6.4 The rules for the ROC

  • The ROC is always a ring centred on the origin, and it never contains a pole.
  • Right-sided signal → the ROC is the outside of the outermost pole.
  • Left-sided signal → the ROC is the inside of the innermost pole.
  • Two-sided signal → the ROC is an annulus between two poles, or empty.
  • Finite-length signal → the ROC is the whole plane, minus z = 0 if it has positive-n terms, minus z = ∞ if it has negative-n terms.
  • A signal that starts at n = 0 has an ROC that includes z = ∞. That is exactly the test for causality.

Worked example

Find X(z) and its ROC for x(n) = (0.5)^n u(n) - (2)^n u(-n-1).

right-sided piece : (0.5)^n u(n) <-> 1/(1 - 0.5 z^-1) , |z| > 0.5 left-sided piece : -(2)^n u(-n-1) <-> 1/(1 - 2 z^-1) , |z| < 2 X(z) = 1/(1 - 0.5 z^-1) + 1/(1 - 2 z^-1) ROC = intersection = 0.5 < |z| < 2

The intersection is not empty, so the signal exists — and the ring contains |z| = 1, so the signal is absolutely summable and the system is stable. It is not causal: it has values at negative n.

Numerical check at z = 1, which lies in the ring: the direct sum gives 2 - 1 = 1, and the closed form gives 1/0.5 + 1/(-1) = 2 - 1 = 1. They also agree at z = 1.3 + 0.4j.

if the second term had been -(0.3)^n u(-n-1): ROC would need |z| > 0.5 AND |z| < 0.3 -> EMPTY the z-transform of that signal does not exist
Exam tip

The phrase “mention the ROC in all cases” appears in this paper every single year. It is asking for several answers from one algebraic X(z), and most of the marks are in the ROC discussion, not in the partial fractions.

Chapter 06 at a glance
  • X(z) = sum x(n)z^-n, and the answer is incomplete without its ROC.
  • a^n u(n) and -a^n u(-n-1) have the identical X(z) and differ only in the ROC — note the minus sign on the left-sided one.
  • ROC is a ring centred on the origin and never contains a pole: right-sided → outside the outermost pole, left-sided → inside the innermost.
  • Two-sided signals give an annulus, which may be empty — and then no z-transform exists.
  • The workhorse property is the delay: x(n-k) <-> z^-k X(z), which turns a difference equation into algebra in one line.
  • Convolution becomes multiplication: x ∗ h <-> X(z)H(z).

Chapter 07

The inverse z-transform

Going back from X(z) to x(n). Three methods exist, but before any of them there is one question: where is the ROC? Get that wrong and every sign in the answer is wrong. This chapter settles partial fractions done safely, and the three-cases question that is worth five marks a year.

7.1 The three methods

MethodUse it whenWhat it gives
Partial fractionsX(z) is a ratio of polynomials — almost alwaysA closed formula for x(n)
Power series / long divisionYou only need the first few samples, or X(z) is not rationalThe samples one at a time, no formula
Contour integral / residuesAsked for by name, or the poles are awkwardx(n) = sum of residues of X(z)z^(n-1) inside the contour

7.2 Partial fractions, done the safe way

Expand X(z)/z, not X(z)

Divide by z first, split into partial fractions, then multiply every term back by z. That leaves each term in the form A*z/(z - p) = A/(1 - p*z^-1), which is exactly the shape in the table. Expanding X(z) directly tends to leave a stray constant that is easy to lose.

The alternative, equally safe, is to treat z^-1 as the variable throughout and match coefficients of 1, z^-1, z^-2 in turn. Use whichever you have practised — but use the same one every time.

7.3 The ROC picks the sequence

Term by term

For each pole p, look at where the ROC sits relative to that pole. If the ROC is outside |z| = |p|, that term is causal: +A*p^n*u(n). If the ROC is inside, that term is anti-causal: -A*p^n*u(-n-1). Different poles in the same X(z) can go different ways — that is what makes a two-sided answer.

Worked example

H(z) = (3 - 4z^-1) / (1 - 3.5z^-1 + 1.5z^-2). Give every possible ROC and h(n) in each case, and say which is causal and which is stable.

Step 1 -- factor the denominator. 1 - 3.5 z^-1 + 1.5 z^-2 = (1 - 3 z^-1)(1 - 0.5 z^-1) poles at z = 3 and z = 0.5 Step 2 -- partial fractions. H(z) = A/(1 - 3z^-1) + B/(1 - 0.5z^-1) 3 - 4z^-1 = A(1 - 0.5z^-1) + B(1 - 3z^-1) constant : A + B = 3 z^-1 : -0.5A - 3B = -4 solving: A = 2 , B = 1 H(z) = 2/(1 - 3z^-1) + 1/(1 - 0.5z^-1)

Step 3 — two poles cut the plane into three rings, and each ring is a different h(n).

|z| > 3 both terms causal h(n) = 2(3)^n u(n) + (0.5)^n u(n) causal YES , stable NO (ring excludes |z| = 1) 0.5 < |z| < 3 inside the pole at 3, outside the pole at 0.5 h(n) = -2(3)^n u(-n-1) + (0.5)^n u(n) causal NO , stable YES (ring contains |z| = 1) |z| < 0.5 both terms anti-causal h(n) = -2(3)^n u(-n-1) - (0.5)^n u(-n-1) causal NO , stable NO

The sentence that earns the last mark: this system cannot be both causal and stable. Causal forces |z| > 3, which excludes the unit circle. Stable forces the annulus, which is two-sided. A causal system is stable only when every pole lies inside |z| = 1, and the pole at z = 3 does not.

The minus sign

Every anti-causal term carries a minus: -A*p^n*u(-n-1), never +A*p^n*u(-n-1). Dropping it is the single most common error in this question, and it is worth a mark on its own.

7.4 Long division, and complex poles

For a causal signal, divide in descending powers of z so the quotient comes out as x(0) + x(1)z^-1 + x(2)z^-2 + .... For an anti-causal one, divide in ascending powers. The coefficients are the samples.

a complex conjugate pole pair at r * e^(+/- j*w0) gives, for a causal signal: x(n) = A * r^n * cos(w0*n + phi) * u(n) the radius r sets how fast it decays, the angle w0 sets how fast it oscillates
Exam tip

With a complex pair you are not expected to produce A and phi from nowhere. Get r and w0 from the poles, write the answer in the r^n (C1 cos + C2 sin) form, and fit C1 and C2 from x(0) and x(1) obtained by long division. Two samples, two unknowns.

Chapter 07 at a glance
  • Before inverting anything, find the ROC. It decides the sign and the side of every term.
  • Partial fractions: expand X(z)/z, split, then multiply back by z — that leaves terms in the A/(1 - p z^-1) shape.
  • Term by term: ROC outside the pole → +A p^n u(n); ROC inside the pole → -A p^n u(-n-1), with the minus sign.
  • M poles at distinct radii give M + 1 possible ROCs and M + 1 different signals from one X(z).
  • Long division in descending powers of z gives a causal sequence; ascending powers gives an anti-causal one.
  • A complex pole pair r e^(+/-jw0) gives r^n cos(w0 n + phi) — radius sets the decay, angle sets the oscillation.

Chapter 08

System function, poles, zeros and stability

H(z) is the difference equation written as one fraction, and everything about the system can be read off where its poles and zeros sit. This chapter settles the two separate tests — causal and stable — and the exact condition under which they can hold at once.

8.1 From difference equation to H(z)

y(n) + sum a_k y(n-k) = sum b_k x(n-k) H(z) = Y(z)/X(z) = ( b0 + b1 z^-1 + ... + bM z^-M ) ------------------------------- ( 1 + a1 z^-1 + ... + aN z^-N ) multiply top and bottom by z^N to read poles and zeros directly
Poles and zeros

Zeros are the roots of the numerator — frequencies the system kills. Poles are the roots of the denominator — the system’s own natural modes. Only the poles decide stability. Zeros never do.

8.2 Reading the pole-zero plot

  • Pole inside the unit circle → a mode that decays. The nearer the origin, the faster.
  • Pole on the unit circle → a mode that neither grows nor decays. Not BIBO stable.
  • Pole outside → a mode that grows without limit.
  • The angle of a pole is the frequency it resonates at; its radius is how sharp the resonance is.
  • A zero sitting on the unit circle at angle ω0 puts an exact null in the response at ω0.

8.3 Causal, stable, or both

PropertyTest on the ROCTest on the poles
CausalROC is the outside of the outermost pole, and includes z = ∞—
StableROC contains the unit circle |z| = 1—
Causal and stable together—Every pole strictly inside the unit circle
These are two separate questions

A system can be stable and not causal. A system can be causal and not stable. They coincide only when every pole is inside |z| = 1 — then the causal ROC already contains the unit circle, so the causal answer is the stable answer.

Worked example

H(z) has three poles, at z = 0.5, z = 2 and z = 5. Comment on causality and stability.

H(z) = 1 / [ (1 - 0.5z^-1)(1 - 2z^-1)(1 - 5z^-1) ] three distinct pole radii -> 3 + 1 = FOUR possible ROCs |z| > 5 causal NOT stable (unit circle is outside the ring) 2 < |z| < 5 two-sided NOT stable 0.5 < |z| < 2 two-sided STABLE (contains |z| = 1) |z| < 0.5 anti-causal NOT stable

Conclusion to write out: with poles outside the unit circle the system cannot be both causal and stable. Exactly one ROC is stable and it is a two-sided one. State which ROC you are assuming before you answer anything else — without it the question has no single answer.

Contrast with H(z) = (3 - 4z^-1)/(1 - z^-1 + 0.5z^-2). The denominator gives z^2 - z + 0.5 = 0, so z = 0.5 +/- 0.5j, a conjugate pair with

|z| = sqrt(0.25 + 0.25) = 0.7071 angle = pi/4

Both poles are inside the unit circle at the same radius, so there are only two ROCs, and the causal one (|z| > 0.707) is also the stable one. Here causal and stable give the identical h(n) — a decaying oscillation (0.707)^n at w0 = pi/4.

Exam tip

Count the distinct pole radii first. M distinct radii means M + 1 possible ROCs, and the question “specify all possible regions of convergence” is asking for exactly that many answers. Two poles at the same radius count as one radius — that is why a complex pair gives two ROCs, not three.

Chapter 08 at a glance
  • H(z) = numerator over denominator; zeros from the top, poles from the bottom, and only poles decide stability.
  • Causal ⇔ ROC is outside the outermost pole. Stable ⇔ ROC contains |z| = 1. Two separate tests.
  • Causal and stable together ⇔ every pole strictly inside the unit circle. Nothing else.
  • M distinct pole radii give M + 1 possible ROCs — a complex conjugate pair is one radius, not two.
  • A pole’s radius sets how fast its mode decays; its angle sets the frequency it resonates at.
  • A zero on the unit circle at angle ω0 puts an exact null in the frequency response at ω0.

Chapter 09

Frequency response

Evaluate H(z) on the unit circle and you get the gain and phase shift the system applies at every frequency. A magnitude-and-phase question appears in every paper. This chapter settles the substitution, the algebraic identity that saves five minutes, and the two-substitution trick for naming the filter type.

9.1 The four Fourier transforms

There are four, and they differ in only two ways: is time continuous or sampled, and does the signal repeat?

Aperiodic signalPeriodic signal
Continuous timeCTFT — spectrum continuous and aperiodicCTFS — spectrum discrete lines, aperiodic
Discrete timeDTFT — spectrum continuous in ω and periodic with period 2πDFS / DFT — spectrum discrete and periodic: N numbers, then they repeat
The rule behind the grid

Discrete in one domain ⇔ periodic in the other. Sampled time → the spectrum repeats every 2π. Sampled frequency → the time signal repeats every N. Only the DFT is discrete and finite in both domains, so only the DFT can be computed.

9.2 The DTFT and the unit circle

X(w) = sum_{n} x(n) e^(-j*w*n) = X(z) evaluated at z = e^(jw) x(n) = (1/(2*pi)) integral_{-pi}^{pi} X(w) e^(j*w*n) dw X(w) is periodic with period 2*pi -- only -pi..pi is distinct
When the substitution is illegal

You may replace z by e^jw only if the ROC contains the unit circle. If a pole sits on or outside |z| = 1 the DTFT does not exist in the ordinary sense, and the honest answer says so.

9.3 Magnitude and phase

The identity that saves five minutes

|1 - a*e^(-jw)|^2 = 1 - 2*a*cos(w) + a^2. With a = 0.5 that is 1.25 - cos(w) straight away, with no expanding of sin² and cos². It appears in every one of these questions.

For a ratio, take magnitudes of top and bottom separately and divide; take angles of top and bottom separately and subtract. Never try to rationalise the whole fraction first.

Worked example

y(n) - 0.5*y(n-1) = x(n). Find the magnitude and phase of the frequency response and say what kind of filter it is.

H(z) = 1 / (1 - 0.5 z^-1) pole at z = 0.5 , zero at z = 0 H(e^jw) = 1 / (1 - 0.5 e^-jw) |1 - 0.5 e^-jw|^2 = 1 - 2(0.5)cos w + 0.25 = 1.25 - cos w |H(e^jw)| = 1 / sqrt(1.25 - cos w) /_H(e^jw) = - arctan[ 0.5 sin w / (1 - 0.5 cos w) ]

Evaluate at three points:

w = 0 : |H| = 1/sqrt(0.25) = 2.0000 phase = 0 w = pi/2 : |H| = 1/sqrt(1.25) = 0.8944 phase = -0.4636 rad w = pi : |H| = 1/sqrt(2.25) = 0.6667 phase = 0

Gain falls from 2 at DC to 0.667 at the highest frequency, so this is a low-pass system with a DC gain of 2. All three values were confirmed against a direct complex evaluation of 1/(1 - 0.5e^-jw).

9.4 Low-pass or high-pass in two substitutions

Two numbers name the filter

The gain at DC is H(z) at z = +1, which is just the sum of all the coefficients. The gain at the highest frequency is H(z) at z = -1. Bigger at +1 → low-pass. Bigger at −1 → high-pass. No plotting needed.

example: H(z) = (1 + z^-1) / (1 - 0.25 z^-1) H(+1) = (1 + 1)/(1 - 0.25) = 2/0.75 = 2.667 <- DC gain H(-1) = (1 - 1)/(1 + 0.25) = 0/1.25 = 0 <- exact null low-pass, and the null is the zero sitting at z = -1
Infinite DC gain

If the denominator vanishes at z = 1 the DC gain is infinite. Say so plainly: there is a pole on the unit circle at DC, the system is not BIBO stable, and it behaves like an accumulator at low frequencies.

9.5 Steady-state response to a sinusoid

Put a sinusoid into a stable LTI system and, once the transient has died, the same sinusoid comes out — only scaled and shifted. This is the single most useful consequence of the frequency response.

in : x(n) = A cos(w0*n + theta) out: y(n) = A |H(w0)| cos( w0*n + theta + angle H(w0) ) the frequency NEVER changes -- only amplitude and phase
Exam tip

Worked on the system above at ω0 = π/3: |H| = 2/√3 = 1.1547 and the phase is −π/6 = −0.5236 rad. Running the recursion for 400 samples gives exactly that amplitude and lag. If a question gives you a sinusoidal input, you do not need to convolve anything — evaluate H at that one frequency.

Chapter 09 at a glance
  • Frequency response = H(z) evaluated at z = e^jw, legal only if the ROC contains the unit circle.
  • The DTFT is continuous in ω and periodic with period 2π — only −π to π is distinct.
  • Use |1 - a e^-jw|^2 = 1 - 2a cos w + a^2; take magnitudes and angles of numerator and denominator separately.
  • Filter type in two substitutions: H(+1) is the DC gain, H(-1) is the gain at ω = π. Bigger at +1 means low-pass.
  • A zero on the unit circle gives an exact null; a pole on it gives infinite gain and no BIBO stability.
  • Steady state: a sinusoid in gives the same frequency out, scaled by |H| and shifted by ∠H.

Chapter 10

The DFT and its properties

The DFT is the only Fourier transform a computer can actually evaluate: N numbers in, N numbers out. A DFT question appears in every paper, usually a small hand computation with a trap attached. This chapter settles the definition, the free checks, the periodicity trap and circular convolution.

10.1 Why the DFT exists

The DTFT X(w) is a continuous function of ω over an infinite set of frequencies. A machine cannot store that. Sample it at N equally spaced points round the unit circle and you get N numbers — the DFT. For a signal of length N or less, nothing at all is lost.

DTFT X(w)DFT X(k)
Frequency variableω, continuousk = 0, 1, ..., N−1, discrete
How many valuesInfinitely manyExactly N
Periodic in the frequency variable?Yes, period 2πYes, period N
Signal it impliesThe signal as given, running to infinityThe signal repeated every N samples
Computable?No — it is a formula, not a listYes — that is the whole point
Relation—X(k) = X(w) at w = 2*pi*k/N, exactly

10.2 The definition and the twiddle factor

W_N = e^(-j*2*pi/N) the twiddle factor X(k) = sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n) W_N^(n*k) k = 0 .. N-1 x(n) = (1/N) sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X(k) W_N^(-n*k) useful symmetries: W_N^(k+N) = W_N^k (periodic) W_N^(k+N/2) = -W_N^k (half-turn flips the sign) W_N^2 = W_(N/2) W_4^k = 1, -j, -1, +j
For N = 4, throw the trigonometry away

W_4^(nk) = (-j)^(nk). Every term is 1, −j, −1 or +j, so a four-point DFT is pure bookkeeping with powers of −j. The paper sets N = 4 almost every year for exactly this reason.

10.3 The properties worth memorising

  • X(0) is the sum of the samples. Every twiddle is 1 at k = 0.
  • Conjugate symmetry. If x(n) is real then X(N-k) = conj(X(k)), so the second half is free. And X(N/2) must be purely real.
  • Circular time shift → linear phase: x((n-m) mod N) <-> W_N^(km) X(k).
  • Frequency shift → circular rotation of the spectrum: x(n) W_N^(-k0*n) <-> X((k - k0) mod N).
  • Circular convolution → multiplication: x(n) (*) h(n) <-> X(k)H(k).
  • Parseval: sum |x(n)|^2 = (1/N) sum |X(k)|^2.
Exam tip

When a frequency-shift question asks which way the spectrum rotates, do not trust memory — write one term out from the definition. Getting the direction backwards is the only real way to lose those marks.

10.4 The periodicity trap

Worked example

x(n) = {1, 2, 3, 4} for n = 0..3. Compute the DFT, then state X(4) through X(7).

N = 4 , so W_4^(nk) = (-j)^(nk) X(0) = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10 X(1) = 1 + 2(-j) + 3(-j)^2 + 4(-j)^3 = 1 -2j -3 +4j = -2 + 2j X(2) = 1 + 2(-j)^2 + 3(-j)^4 + 4(-j)^6 = 1 -2 +3 -4 = -2 X(3) = 1 + 2(-j)^3 + 3(-j)^6 + 4(-j)^9 = 1 +2j -3 -4j = -2 - 2j X(k) = { 10 , -2+2j , -2 , -2-2j }

Two free checks. X(0) = 1+2+3+4 = 10 ✓. And x(n) is real, so X(3) must be the conjugate of X(1): -2-2j against -2+2j ✓. X(2) sits at N/2 and must be purely real — it is ✓.

X(4) = X(0) = 10 X(5) = X(1) = -2 + 2j X(6) = X(2) = -2 X(7) = X(3) = -2 - 2j

Confirmed against numpy: the 4-point DFT is exactly [10, -2+2j, -2, -2-2j], and the inverse transform returns 1, 2, 3, 4.

Being asked for eight values does not make it an 8-point DFT

A 4-point DFT has exactly four distinct values and then repeats for ever. An 8-point DFT would need eight samples, or an explicit instruction to zero-pad to 8 — and that would give genuinely different numbers, not repeats. This exact trap has been set twice in three years.

10.5 Circular convolution, and zero padding

Multiplying two DFTs does not give the ordinary convolution. It gives the circular one, where the index wraps round instead of running off the end. The tails that fall off the end come back at the beginning and add.

circular: y(n) = sum_{k=0}^{N-1} x(k) h( (n-k) mod N ) to get the LINEAR answer from a circular convolution, zero-pad both signals to length L >= N + M - 1
Worked example

x(n) = {1, 2, 3, 4} and h(n) = {1, 1, 0, 0}, both of length N = 4. Do the circular convolution and compare it with the linear one.

y(0) = x0 h0 + x1 h3 + x2 h2 + x3 h1 = 1 + 0 + 0 + 4 = 5 y(1) = x0 h1 + x1 h0 + x2 h3 + x3 h2 = 1 + 2 + 0 + 0 = 3 y(2) = x0 h2 + x1 h1 + x2 h0 + x3 h3 = 0 + 2 + 3 + 0 = 5 y(3) = x0 h3 + x1 h2 + x2 h1 + x3 h0 = 0 + 0 + 3 + 4 = 7 circular y(n) = { 5, 3, 5, 7 }

Now the linear convolution of {1,2,3,4} with {1,1}:

linear = { 1, 3, 5, 7, 4 } length 4 + 2 - 1 = 5 the 5th sample has nowhere to go in a 4-point circle, so it wraps round and adds to the first: { 1+4 , 3 , 5 , 7 } = { 5, 3, 5, 7 } matches exactly

Pad both to length 5 or more and the wraparound disappears: the circular answer then equals the linear one. Both results were confirmed numerically.

What zero padding does and does not do

It does put the DFT points closer together, so the plot looks smoother, and it does let circular convolution give the linear answer. It does not improve frequency resolution. Resolution needs a longer record, not more zeros. Bin spacing is F_k = k*Fs/N.

  • X(k) = sum x(n) W_N^(nk) with W_N = e^(-j2pi/N); for N = 4 that is just powers of −j.
  • Two free checks on every DFT: X(0) = sum x(n), and for real x(n) X(N-k) = conj(X(k)) with X(N/2) purely real.
  • The DFT is periodic in k with period N — asking for X(4)..X(7) of a 4-point DFT is asking for X(0)..X(3) again.
  • Multiplying two DFTs gives circular convolution; the tail wraps round and adds to the front.
  • Zero-pad both signals to L >= N + M - 1 and circular convolution equals the linear answer.
  • Zero padding adds dots, not resolution. Resolution needs a longer record; bin spacing is Fs/N.

Chapter 11

The FFT

The FFT is not a different transform — it is the same DFT computed by splitting it in half over and over. This is the most reliably examined topic in the paper and the one that moves around most. This chapter settles both decimations, the counting formulas, bit reversal, and what changes for a different radix.

11.1 What direct computation costs

direct DFT: complex multiplications = N^2 complex additions = N(N - 1) radix-2 FFT: complex multiplications = (N/2) log2 N complex additions = N log2 N stages = log2 N butterflies per stage = N/2
The two symmetries that make it work

W_N^(k+N) = W_N^k (periodicity) and W_N^(k+N/2) = -W_N^k (a half-turn flips the sign). Between them, half of every product has already been computed — that is the entire saving, and it is the sentence to open the answer with.

11.2 Radix-2 decimation in time

Split x(n) into its even-indexed and odd-indexed samples. Each half is an N/2-point DFT, and the two are recombined with one twiddle multiplication per output pair. Repeat the split until only 2-point DFTs are left.

X(k) = G(k) + W_N^k H(k) X(k + N/2) = G(k) - W_N^k H(k) k = 0 .. N/2 - 1 G(k) = DFT of the even-indexed samples H(k) = DFT of the odd-indexed samples

11.3 Bit reversal and in-place working

Splitting into evens and odds over and over leaves the input in bit-reversed order. Write each index in log2 N bits and read the bits backwards.

N = 8, three bits: 0 = 000 -> 000 = 0 4 = 100 -> 001 = 1 1 = 001 -> 100 = 4 5 = 101 -> 101 = 5 2 = 010 -> 010 = 2 6 = 110 -> 011 = 3 3 = 011 -> 110 = 6 7 = 111 -> 111 = 7 DIT input order : x(0) x(4) x(2) x(6) x(1) x(5) x(3) x(7) DIT output order: X(0) X(1) X(2) X(3) X(4) X(5) X(6) X(7)
In-place computation

Each butterfly reads two values, computes two results, and writes them back over the same two locations. No second array is needed — total storage is about 5N/2 words (2N for the complex data plus N/2 for the twiddles). Bit reversal is the price paid for working in place, and it is worth a mark to say so.

11.4 Radix-2 decimation in frequency

The same saving from the other end: split the output into even-numbered and odd-numbered frequencies instead of splitting the input into even and odd samples. Stage 1 combines samples that are N/2 apart, stage 2 combines samples N/4 apart, and so on down to adjacent pairs.

11.5 Counting the operations

Worked example

Compare direct computation with the radix-2 FFT, at N = 8 and at N = 1024.

Why other radices exist at all

Radix-2 needs N to be a power of two. If your record is naturally 9 or 27 samples long, padding up to 16 or 32 changes the frequency grid. Radix-3 keeps the natural length. Every radix is the same divide-and-conquer idea with a different number, and every one of them can be computed in place.

  • Radix-2 costs (N/2)log2 N multiplications and N log2 N additions, in log2 N stages of N/2 butterflies — against N^2 and N(N-1) direct.

12.2 The one that moves

The FFT question is the unstable one. 2023 asked for a radix-3 flow graph for N = 9. 2024 asked for operation counts including split-radix, and separately for DIT applied to the inverse DFT. 2025 asked for decimation in frequency on 16 points.

So do not learn only the 8-point DIT butterfly

Learn the counting formulas, both decimations, what changes for the inverse, and what changes for a different radix. Four small things instead of one big drawing, and they cover every version the question has taken.

12.4 Ninety minutes, spent

  1. First five minutes — read all five questions and start with the DFT or FFT one. It is the most mechanical and the most certain marks in the paper.
  2. Then the difference equation. If the question says “using homogeneous and particular solution” you must show both parts — the z-transform alone scores nothing. But do the z-transform on rough paper anyway: two minutes, and it catches the arithmetic slips where these marks are actually lost.
  3. The z-transform question — factor, partial-fraction, then spend most of your words on the ROC cases. Write the causal-and-stable sentence out in full.
  4. The frequency response — substitute z = e^jw, use |1 - a e^-jw|^2 = 1 - 2a cos w + a^2, then evaluate at ω = 0 and ω = π and name the filter.
  5. Last ten minutes — run the free checks. Sum check on every convolution, X(0) = sum x(n) on every DFT, three samples of the recursion against every closed-form response.
Assume and say so

The whole subject in one screen

Everything, condensed

01Signals, and what discrete time changes
  • Digital frequency f = F/Fs; only −½ ≤ f ≤ ½ is distinct, and ω and ω + 2πk are the same signal.
  • A discrete sinusoid is periodic only if f is a ratio of whole numbers; write f = k/N in lowest terms and N is the period.
  • For a sum of sinusoids the period is the LCM of the individual periods.
  • Energy signal ⇒ E finite and P = 0. Power signal ⇒ E infinite and P finite. Every periodic signal is a power signal.
  • The fastest discrete oscillation is at ω = π: one sign change per sample. There is nothing faster.
02Sampling, aliasing and quantization
  • Sample faster than twice the highest frequency: Fs > 2*Fmax. The Nyquist rate is 2*Fmax; the folding frequency is Fs/2.
  • Every frequency in the family F0 + k*Fs gives identical samples — that is aliasing.
  • To find the apparent frequency: form f = F/Fs, slide it into −½ ≤ f ≤ ½ by whole numbers, multiply back by Fs.
  • Aliasing is not recoverable: two components can land on the same digital frequency and add.
  • Quantization step D = (x_max - x_min)/(L-1), error at most D/2, and SQNR = 1.76 + 6.02b dB — about 6 dB per bit.
03Discrete-time signals: writing them, moving them
  • Four notations for one signal — functional, tabular, sequence, graphical. In sequence form the arrow marks n = 0 and must be carried everywhere.
  • d(n) = u(n) - u(n-1), and x(n) = sum_k x(k)d(n-k) — any signal is a row of weighted impulses. That identity is where convolution comes from.
  • x(n-k) delays to the right for positive k; x(-n) mirrors about the origin.
  • Fold and shift do not commute: rewrite as x(-(n+k)) first, then fold, then shift by what is left in the bracket.
  • Even part (x(n)+x(-n))/2, odd part (x(n)-x(-n))/2, and the odd part is always zero at n = 0.
04LTI systems and convolution
  • For an LTI system, h(n) is everything: causal ⇔ h(n) = 0 for n < 0, stable ⇔ sum |h(n)| is finite. FIR is always stable.
  • y(n) = sum_k x(k)h(n-k) — break the input into impulses, add the answers.
  • Result length = n1 + n2 − 1, starting at (start of x + start of h).
  • Free check every time: sum y = (sum x)(sum h).
  • Cascade multiplies the responses (h1 ∗ h2); parallel adds them (h1 + h2).
  • Step response is the running sum of h; so h(n) = s(n) - s(n-1) recovers the system from its step response.
05Difference equations and the total response
  • No feedback → FIR and always stable; feedback → IIR and the roots decide stability.
  • Total = natural + forced = zero-input + zero-state — two different cuts of the same answer, and zero-state still contains a transient.
  • Homogeneous: solve lambda^N + a1*lambda^(N-1) + ... = 0; particular: guess the shape of the input; then fit the constants from y(0), y(1) computed by the equation.
  • Impulse response: homogeneous solution with h0(0) = 1, then h(n) = b0*h0(n) + b1*h0(n-1) + ....
  • The inverse system swaps x and y — and swaps poles with zeros, so an IIR system inverts to an FIR one.
  • Always verify a closed-form answer by running the recursion for three samples. It takes thirty seconds.
06The z-transform and the ROC
  • X(z) = sum x(n)z^-n, and the answer is incomplete without its ROC.
  • a^n u(n) and -a^n u(-n-1) have the identical X(z) and differ only in the ROC — note the minus sign on the left-sided one.
  • ROC is a ring centred on the origin and never contains a pole: right-sided → outside the outermost pole, left-sided → inside the innermost.
  • Two-sided signals give an annulus, which may be empty — and then no z-transform exists.
  • The workhorse property is the delay: x(n-k) <-> z^-k X(z), which turns a difference equation into algebra in one line.
  • Convolution becomes multiplication: x ∗ h <-> X(z)H(z).
07The inverse z-transform
  • Before inverting anything, find the ROC. It decides the sign and the side of every term.
  • Partial fractions: expand X(z)/z, split, then multiply back by z — that leaves terms in the A/(1 - p z^-1) shape.
  • Term by term: ROC outside the pole → +A p^n u(n); ROC inside the pole → -A p^n u(-n-1), with the minus sign.
  • M poles at distinct radii give M + 1 possible ROCs and M + 1 different signals from one X(z).
  • Long division in descending powers of z gives a causal sequence; ascending powers gives an anti-causal one.
  • A complex pole pair r e^(+/-jw0) gives r^n cos(w0 n + phi) — radius sets the decay, angle sets the oscillation.
08System function, poles, zeros and stability
  • H(z) = numerator over denominator; zeros from the top, poles from the bottom, and only poles decide stability.
  • Causal ⇔ ROC is outside the outermost pole. Stable ⇔ ROC contains |z| = 1. Two separate tests.
  • Causal and stable together ⇔ every pole strictly inside the unit circle. Nothing else.
  • M distinct pole radii give M + 1 possible ROCs — a complex conjugate pair is one radius, not two.
  • A pole’s radius sets how fast its mode decays; its angle sets the frequency it resonates at.
  • A zero on the unit circle at angle ω0 puts an exact null in the frequency response at ω0.
09Frequency response
  • Frequency response = H(z) evaluated at z = e^jw, legal only if the ROC contains the unit circle.
  • The DTFT is continuous in ω and periodic with period 2π — only −π to π is distinct.
  • Use |1 - a e^-jw|^2 = 1 - 2a cos w + a^2; take magnitudes and angles of numerator and denominator separately.
  • Filter type in two substitutions: H(+1) is the DC gain, H(-1) is the gain at ω = π. Bigger at +1 means low-pass.
  • A zero on the unit circle gives an exact null; a pole on it gives infinite gain and no BIBO stability.
  • Steady state: a sinusoid in gives the same frequency out, scaled by |H| and shifted by ∠H.
10The DFT and its properties
  • X(k) = sum x(n) W_N^(nk) with W_N = e^(-j2pi/N); for N = 4 that is just powers of −j.
  • Two free checks on every DFT: X(0) = sum x(n), and for real x(n) X(N-k) = conj(X(k)) with X(N/2) purely real.
  • The DFT is periodic in k with period N — asking for X(4)..X(7) of a 4-point DFT is asking for X(0)..X(3) again.
  • Multiplying two DFTs gives circular convolution; the tail wraps round and adds to the front.
  • Zero-pad both signals to L >= N + M - 1 and circular convolution equals the linear answer.
  • Zero padding adds dots, not resolution. Resolution needs a longer record; bin spacing is Fs/N.
