Chapter 01

The Whole Link, in One Diagram

Every other chapter here is a zoom into one box of a single diagram. This chapter puts that diagram on the page and gives each block a one-line job. It also settles the three numbers — BER, SNR and Eb/N0 — that decide whether a design is any good, and answers the one question examiners ask about this diagram every single year.

1.1 What the system is for

Somebody wants to send a voice, a photo or a sensor reading to somebody far away. In between sits a channel — air, copper, fibre, space. The channel weakens the signal, adds noise, bends it out of shape, and lets other people’s signals crash into it. The whole subject is one sentence: send information reliably and efficiently across a channel that is not kind.

Why go digital at all

A damaged analog signal stays damaged, and every amplifier on the route makes it a little worse. A digital signal is only ever 0 or 1, so as long as the noise is not big enough to flip one into the other, the receiver rebuilds it perfectly. And because it is only 0s and 1s, you can compress it, encrypt it and add error-correcting maths to it.

The mirror rule

Every receiver block exactly undoes the matching transmitter block, in reverse order. Source encoder pairs with source decoder, channel encoder with channel decoder, modulator with demodulator. Say this sentence when you finish the diagram — it is a mark on its own.

The question they ask every year

“Source coding removes redundancy but channel coding adds it. Is that not a contradiction?” No. Source coding strips out useless, accidental redundancy that was already sitting in the data. Channel coding then adds back a small, mathematically designed amount, built to fight noise. Throw out the junk, then add the right tool.

BER is the report card. A BER of 10-6 means one bit in a million comes out wrong. Eb is the energy spent on one bit; N0 is the noise power in each hertz of bandwidth. So Eb/N0 asks how loud one bit is compared with the hiss. Higher Eb/N0 always means lower BER.

1.4 AWGN, four letters

  • Additive — the noise simply adds: y(t) = x(t) + n(t). It does not multiply or twist the signal.
  • White — flat across the whole spectrum, like white light containing every colour.
  • Gaussian — the voltage at any instant follows the bell curve. Small values common, huge values rare.
  • Noise — unwanted random energy, mostly electrons jiggling because of heat.

Chapter 02

Sampling and Quantisation

This is the A/D block opened up, and it is the most numerical-heavy topic on the paper. Three steps turn a smooth wave into bits, and each step has one formula. The chapter settles how fast you must sample, how much error quantising costs, and which of the two SQNR formulas to use — getting that choice wrong is the commonest mistake in the subject.

2.1 The three steps of PCM

  1. Sampling — measure the signal’s height at regular times.
  2. Quantisation — round each measured height to the nearest allowed level.
  3. Encoding — write each level number as a binary codeword.

2.2 Nyquist — how often to sample

fs≥2fmfs=sampling rate,fm=highest frequency in the signal2fm is called the Nyquist rate\begin{aligned} f_s &\ge 2 f_m f_s = \text{sampling rate}, f_m = \text{highest frequency in the signal} \\ 2 f_m\, \text{is called the Nyquist rate} & \end{aligned}fs​2fm​is called the Nyquist rate​≥2fm​fs​=sampling rate,fm​=highest frequency in the signal​

You must take at least two samples in every cycle of the fastest wiggle: one for the peak, one for the trough. Fewer than two and a fast wave can pretend to be a slow one, and you can never tell them apart afterwards. That fault is aliasing.

Telephone speech is limited to about 4 kHz, so telephones sample at 8 kHz. Music reaches 20 kHz, so CDs sample at 44.1 kHz — a little above 2 × 20 kHz, to leave room for the filter.

Cut the full voltage swing into 2n equal slices. Because you always round to the nearest level, the worst you can ever be wrong is half a step. The step-squared-over-twelve comes from statistics: if the error is equally likely to be anywhere between −step/2 and +step/2, its average power is exactly that. Memorise it; the derivation is not asked.

The trap

Use 6.02n + 1.76 only when the question says the signal is a sine wave filling the whole range. The moment a question hands you an actual Vrms value, it wants the other formula. Mixing them up is the most common mistake in this topic, and the two answers differ by tens of decibels.

The headline worth carrying into the hall: every extra bit buys about 6 dB. Double the number of levels and the quantisation noise drops by 6 dB.

Why only 27 dB here when the TV question gave 56 dB? Because this signal is quiet. A full-scale sine across 4 Vpp would have Vrms = 1.414 V; this one has 0.2 V, seven times smaller, so it sits far closer to the quantisation noise floor. That gap is exactly what companding exists to close — Chapter 3.

The five-move attack plan

Almost every PCM numerical is the same five moves in the same order. 1. fs from fm. 2. n — from L, or from an accuracy requirement, or from Rb/fs. 3. the step size from Vpp/2n. 4. Rb = n fs, times N if it is TDM. 5. SNR by whichever of the two formulas fits.

Which way to round n

When n comes from a channel rate limit, round down — you may not exceed the channel. When n comes from an accuracy requirement, round up — you must be at least that accurate. Getting this backwards costs the whole question.

  • Quantising: L = 2n levels, step = Vpp/2n, worst error = step/2, noise power Pq = step²/12.
  • SQNR for a full-scale sine is 6.02n + 1.76 dB; when Vrms is given, use Vrms² divided by step²/12 instead.
  • Every extra bit of the codeword buys about 6 dB of signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio.
  • n from a channel rate limit rounds down; n from an accuracy requirement rounds up.

Chapter 03

PCM Rates, Companding and Delta Modulation

Chapter 2 made the bits. This one turns them into a rate and a bandwidth, shares a wire between several signals, and then fixes the complaint that ended Chapter 2 — that a quiet signal gets a terrible SQNR. The fix is companding. Delta modulation, which has appeared on two of the four papers, closes the chapter.

3.1 Bit rate, bandwidth and TDM

Bit rate is samples per second multiplied by bits per sample. Nothing clever. TDM means several signals share one wire by taking turns: signal 1 sends a sample, then signal 2, and so on. With N signals the shared line carries N × fs samples per second.

An RZ pulse is only half a bit wide, so it wiggles twice as fast, so it needs twice the bandwidth of NRZ. That is the whole reason for the two bandwidth lines above.

3.3 Why uniform quantising is unfair

A uniform quantiser puts its steps at equal spacing all the way up. A loud signal uses all of them and gets a fine SQNR. A quiet signal wanders over two or three steps, so its rounding error is a large fraction of it and the SQNR collapses. Speech is mostly quiet, with rare loud peaks, so this is exactly the wrong behaviour.

a sine at A dB below full scale, uniform quantiser: SQNR_dB = 6.02n + 1.76 - A so an 8-bit uniform quantiser gives 49.92 dB at full scale, but only 19.92 dB on a signal 30 dB down.
Companding, in one line

Compress before quantising, expand after reconstructing. The compressor bends the amplitude scale so that small signals are stretched out over many steps and large ones are squeezed into few. Uniform steps on the bent scale are fine near zero and coarse near full scale when mapped back — which holds SQNR roughly constant over a wide range of signal levels.

Chapter 04

Line Codes — the Six You Must Draw

You have bits; now put them on a wire as voltages. A waveform question has appeared on half the papers and it is worth five marks, most of which are for a tidy grid. This chapter gives the rules for the six codes, the four faults a line code exists to prevent, and the two one-word answers the question always ends with.

4.1 Data element against signal element

  • Data element — the smallest piece of information. That is a bit.
  • Signal element — the shortest unit, timewise, of the actual signal.
  • r — how many data elements ride on one signal element.

Think of signal elements as envelopes and data elements as letters. r = 1 is one letter per envelope. r = 2 stuffs two letters in one envelope: fewer envelopes, less bandwidth, but the levels sit closer together and noise hurts more. r = ½ splits one letter over two envelopes: wasteful, but the extra envelope gives a guaranteed transition for the clock. Manchester is exactly that.

4.2 The four faults a line code must prevent

  1. Baseline wandering. The receiver judges each bit against a running average of the received power. A long run of 1s drags that average up, a long run of 0s drags it down, and once it has moved the receiver compares against the wrong reference.
  2. Loss of synchronisation. If the receiver clock does not tick at the sender rate it samples in the wrong places and reads bits that were never sent.
  3. DC components. A voltage that holds steady puts energy at very low frequencies. Transformer-coupled links and telephone lines cannot pass them, so that part of the signal is simply thrown away.
  4. No self-synchronisation. The timing must be carried inside the data itself, by transitions that mark the start, middle or end of each bit.
The desirable-properties answer

Turn the four faults into requirements and you have a ready-made list-of-six answer: no DC component, no baseline wandering, self-synchronising, small bandwidth, built-in error detection, and low complexity with good noise immunity.

Worked example — getting AMI right first time

The two answers the question always wants

Which detects errors? Bipolar AMI. Successive 1s must alternate in polarity, so two +A pulses in a row with no −A between them is an AMI violation and an error. No extra bits were spent to get it. Which synchronises the clock? Manchester. There is a guaranteed transition in the middle of every bit whatever the data, so the clock is reset every bit period. That is what the doubled bandwidth buys.

Where the marks actually go

Rule the bit boundaries first and write the bits above them. Mark the half-bit points for the RZ rows. A tidy grid is most of the mark and it is the easiest mark on the paper to drop. Manchester also has two conventions in circulation, so state which one you are using in a single line.

Chapter 04 at a glance
  • Line codes exist to stop four faults: baseline wandering, loss of synchronisation, DC content, and no self-clocking.
  • NRZ holds the level all bit; RZ returns to zero mid-bit; unipolar uses one polarity, polar two, bipolar three levels.
  • AMI sends 0 as nothing and alternates the polarity of successive 1s — pencil the polarities before drawing.
  • Manchester puts a transition in the middle of every bit, so it is fully self-synchronising, at double the bandwidth.
  • AMI detects errors free, through bipolar violations; Manchester recovers the clock. Those are the two one-word answers.

Chapter 05

Baud, Bandwidth and Spectra

Bit rate and baud rate are not the same number, and the paper has asked for both from the same waveform. This chapter fixes the difference, derives the Nyquist maximum data rate from it, and then reads the power spectral density plot that answers almost every compare-the-line-codes question in one picture.

5.1 Data rate against signal rate

N = data rate, bits per second (bps) S = signal rate, signal elements per second (baud) S = c . N . (1/r) baud c = case factor, between 0 and 1; take c = 1/2 for the average case r = data elements per signal element

The signal rate is not one fixed number, because it depends on the data pattern. NRZ-I only changes level when a 1 arrives: send all zeros and it never changes at all. So c is a fudge factor saying which case you are in, and unless the question says otherwise it is ½.

5.3 The spectra

  • NRZ is at its maximum at f = 0. A big value at zero frequency means a big DC component. Its first null is at fT = 1, so it is narrow — cheap in spectrum but DC-heavy.
  • Bipolar AMI is zero at f = 0 and humps around fT ≈ 0.5. No DC, and the bandwidth is no worse than NRZ. Best of both, which is why AMI ran the telephone trunk network.
  • Manchester is zero at DC too, but the hump sits near fT ≈ 0.75 and stretches wider. Perfect clock recovery, at roughly double the bandwidth.

AMI’s one weakness, and the cure

AMI sends a 0 as nothing at all, so a long run of zeros is a long silence and the receiver’s clock drifts. Why NRZ-I rather than NRZ-L

NRZ-L is absolute: the level tells you the bit. Swap the two wires anywhere in the cable and every bit comes out inverted. NRZ-I is differential: the bit is carried by whether the level changed, so swapping the wires changes nothing. That immunity to polarity reversal is why differential schemes are used in practice, and why differential Manchester — polarity-proof and self-clocking — was chosen for Token Ring.

Chapter 06

ISI and the Nyquist Criteria

Every real channel has limited bandwidth, and that single fact causes one disease: intersymbol interference. This chapter is the diagnosis and the cure. It opened the September 2025 paper and it opens our predicted paper, so the four marks here are close to guaranteed.

6.1 What ISI is and why it happens

What it is. ISI is the smearing of one transmitted symbol into the time slots of its neighbours, so the value sampled for one bit is contaminated by the tails of the bits around it.

Why it happens. A sharp rectangular pulse contains frequencies out to infinity. Pass it through a channel that removes the high ones and it spreads out in time. The pulse that arrives is wider than the one that was sent, so its tail is still present when the next symbol is sampled.

The picture that earns the mark

Shout a word in a large empty hall. The echo of the first word is still bouncing when you say the second, and the listener hears a muddle. ISI is exactly that, in electrical form — and it gets worse the faster you talk, which is why ISI is what ultimately limits the bit rate.

ISI is not noise

No random hiss was added. The signal interfered with itself, purely because the channel was too narrow. So you cannot fix it by shouting louder — turn up the power and the ISI grows in exactly the same proportion. On a quiet wire such as a telephone line, the system is limited by ISI rather than by noise.

6.2 The ISI equation

y(ti) = u.ai + u . SUM(k =/= i) ak . p[(i-k)Tb] + n(ti) ^^^^ ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^ ^^^^^ wanted ISI noise

Three terms. The first is the bit you actually wanted. The second, with the condition k ≠ i, is every other bit leaking a little of its tail into this sampling instant — that is the ISI. The third is noise. The design goal for the rest of this chapter is to make the middle term exactly zero.

6.3 Nyquist’s first criterion

Nyquist does not say stop the pulses overlapping. He accepts that they will. He insists only that at the exact instants when the receiver looks, the interference happens to be zero. It is a room full of people talking, where everyone else happens to pause at the moment your friend says each word.

The pulse must be tall at its own sampling instant and exactly zero at every other one. Between those instants it may wander, ring and overshoot as much as it likes — nobody is looking then.

The folding test, done with scissors

Draw the pulse spectrum P(f). Make copies shifted left and right by the symbol rate 1/T, 2/T and so on. Stack all the copies and add them up. If the total is a flat horizontal line, the pulse has zero ISI. Wherever one copy falls, its neighbour must rise by the same amount — that property is called odd symmetry about the band edge, and it is the whole secret.

6.6 The second criterion, in one paragraph

The first criterion protects the pulse centres, so the amplitude you read is right. The second protects the pulse edges — the zero crossings — because that is where the receiver gets its timing from. It requires p(t) = 0.5 at t = ±T/2. If ISI moves the crossings around, the recovered clock jitters, and a jittery clock samples in the wrong place, undoing everything the first criterion achieved. The raised cosine at α = 1 satisfies both.

Chapter 07

The Eye, the Equaliser and Duobinary

Chapter 6 was theory. This chapter is how the theory is checked, patched and then deliberately inverted. The eye diagram shows ISI on a scope in two seconds; the zero-forcing equaliser cancels what the channel still ruins; and duobinary throws the whole idea into reverse by adding controlled ISI on purpose. Duobinary was a three-mark question in 2025.

7.1 The eye diagram

Take the received waveform, chop it into pieces one symbol long, and lay every piece on top of every other. A clean channel makes every piece follow nearly the same path, leaving a big gap in the middle that looks like an eye. ISI makes the pieces take many paths, the traces spread, and the eye closes. On a real scope you do not chop anything — you trigger at the symbol rate and let the traces pile up.

  1. Optimum sampling instant — the point of maximum eye opening. Sample here.
  2. Noise margin — the vertical opening at that point. How much noise you can take before an error.
  3. Slope — how sensitive the link is to sampling time. Steep sides mean a small timing error hurts a lot.
  4. Level-crossing ambiguity — the spread of the zero crossings. This is the timing jitter.
  5. Distortion at the sampling point — how much the trace has thickened. This is the ISI.
  6. Error-free sampling region — the horizontal width of the eye. How much timing error you can tolerate.

7.2 The zero-forcing equaliser

A transversal filter is a chain of delay boxes with a multiplier on each tap, all feeding one adder. Feed the received pulse in and choose the 2N + 1 tap weights so that the output is 1 at the wanted sampling instant and forced to zero at the N sampling instants either side. That is the whole idea.

It cancels ISI at those sampling instants exactly, but it does so by inverting the channel — and where the channel response is small, the inverse is large. So a zero-forcing equaliser amplifies the noise in the weak parts of the band. It also only zeroes 2N of the neighbours, not all of them.

7.3 Duobinary — controlled ISI on purpose

Also called correlative coding or partial response signalling. Instead of fighting ISI, deliberately introduce a known amount of it and subtract it back at the receiver. The relaxed condition is p(nT) = 1 for n = 0 and n = 1, zero elsewhere — the interference is limited to the two adjacent samples, and because it is deterministic it can be removed.

8.2 The matched filter

A known pulse s(t) of duration T arrives buried in white Gaussian noise. You get to choose one linear filter, and you will sample its output once, at t = T. Which filter makes that one sample as far above the noise as possible? The answer is the matched filter, and it is unique.

The three facts to state

One. The matched filter maximises the output SNR at the sampling instant, and nothing else can beat it. Two. That maximum is 2E/N0 — it depends only on the pulse energy, not on its shape, so a tall short pulse and a low long one of the same energy do equally well. Three. It is exactly equivalent to a correlator: multiply by a stored copy of s(t), integrate over one symbol, sample at T.

What it does not do

A matched filter does not preserve the shape of the waveform — its output is the autocorrelation of the pulse, which looks nothing like the pulse. It only guarantees the best possible value at one instant, t = T. Sample anywhere else and the guarantee is gone, which is why timing recovery and the matched filter always travel together.

Chapter 09

Information and Entropy

Here the subject becomes beautiful. Shannon asked whether you can put a number on how much information a message carries, and the answer built the digital world. This chapter settles what information means, how to average it into entropy, and how to turn entropy into a bit rate. Entropy or information rate has appeared on three of the four papers.

9.1 Information is surprise

Information depends only on the probability of the event. Not on its content, meaning or importance. A message about the least likely event carries the most information.

Two messages sent to you in Surat in June. “It rained today” — it is the monsoon, you expected it, this tells you almost nothing. “It snowed today” — astonishing, you would call everyone you know. Both are four words and cost the same to send. One surprised you.

#AxiomWhy it must hold
1I(p) ≥ 0You can never learn a negative amount.
2I(1) = 0An event that was certain tells you nothing when it happens.
3I(p1p2) = I(p1) + I(p2)Two independent pieces of news should add.
4I(p) continuous and decreasing in pRarer always means more, with no sudden jumps.

Only one function satisfies all four: the logarithm of 1/p. Axiom 3 is the decisive one, because the logarithm is the function that turns multiplication into addition. If a question says “justify the logarithmic measure of information”, that list is the answer.

Source Coding — Shannon–Fano, Huffman, Kraft

This is the chapter to learn if you learn only one. Source coding has appeared on every single paper we have, and in 2025 it was both methods as two separate five-mark questions — a third of the whole paper. The procedures are mechanical. The whole trick in one line

Give short codes to common symbols and long codes to rare symbols. Morse worked this out in 1837: E is the commonest English letter and got a single dot; Q is rare and got four symbols. A fixed-length code wastes bits on the common cases, and the common cases are most of your data.

The source coding theorem is a wall. A source with entropy 2.36 bits/symbol cannot be represented in fewer than 2.36 bits per symbol on average — not by ZIP, not by anything invented in future. But you can get arbitrarily close to it, so a good code lands within a fraction of a percent.

10.2 Classifying a code

PropertyWhat it means
Fixed-lengthEvery codeword has the same number of bits.
DistinctNo two symbols share the same codeword. Fail this and the code is useless.
Prefix-freeNo codeword is the beginning of another codeword.
Uniquely decodableThe original message can always be recovered exactly.
InstantaneousYou know a codeword has ended the moment it ends, without peeking ahead.
OptimalInstantaneous, and with the smallest possible L for those probabilities.

Why prefix-free matters. Take the code 0, 1, 00, 11 and receive the bits 0 0. Is that the third symbol, or the first symbol twice? You cannot tell, because 0 is the prefix of 00. Prefix-free means the instant you have a valid codeword you know it is complete, so you decode bit by bit with no waiting.

The subtlety that catches people

Prefix-free is sufficient for unique decodability but not necessary. The code 0, 01, 011, 0111 is not prefix-free, yet it is uniquely decodable: every codeword starts with a 0 and no codeword contains a 0 anywhere else, so a 0 marks where each one begins. It is not instantaneous though — you must wait for the next 0 before you can commit.

10.3 The Kraft inequality

K = SUM 2^(-ni) <= 1 necessary AND sufficient for an instantaneous binary code with those codeword lengths to exist. K = 1 exactly -> the code is complete, no branch of the tree wasted.

Think of it as a budget of 1. A codeword of length n costs 2-n of it: a 1-bit codeword costs a half, a 2-bit one a quarter, a 3-bit one an eighth. Short codewords are expensive. Spend 1 or less and the code is buildable; spend more and the codewords would be forced to collide.

What Kraft does not tell you

It says an instantaneous code with those lengths exists. It does not hand you the codewords, and it does not mean any arbitrary code satisfying it is automatically uniquely decodable. It is an existence test, not a construction method — to actually build the code, use Shannon–Fano or Huffman.

10.4 Shannon–Fano — split from the top

  1. List the symbols in decreasing order of probability.
  2. Split the list into two groups whose totals are as close to equal as possible. Assign 0 to the upper group, 1 to the lower.
  3. Repeat the split inside each group until every group holds one symbol.
  4. Read each codeword by collecting the bits it picked up, left to right.

10.5 Huffman — merge from the bottom

  1. List the symbols in decreasing order of probability.
  2. Add the two smallest probabilities together and put the sum back in the list, re-sorting. That is one reduction.
  3. Repeat until only two probabilities remain.
  4. Assign 0 to one of the last two and 1 to the other, then work backwards: every time you meet a probability you had created by combining, split it and append 0 to one branch and 1 to the other.
  5. Read each codeword by tracing from the root out to the symbol.

Huffman codes are not unique

Three things are free choices: which branch you call 0, how you break a tie between equal probabilities, and where a merged value is placed among equals when you re-sort. What is not free is L. Whichever method is asked for, end with the same five things: L, H(X), η = H/L, redundancy 1 − η, and the Kraft sum. The 2025 paper asked for the Kraft check explicitly. Writing the codewords alone is half an answer.

  • Always report five things: L = ∑P n, H(X), η = H/L, redundancy 1 − η, and the Kraft sum ∑2-n ≤ 1.
  • The source coding theorem sets a hard floor: L ≥ H(X). No code, present or future, beats entropy.
  • Prefix-free means no codeword starts another, which makes decoding instantaneous; it is sufficient but not necessary for unique decodability.
  • Shannon–Fano splits top-down into near-equal halves; Huffman merges the two smallest bottom-up and is always optimal.
  • Huffman codewords are not unique, but L always is — matching L means matching answers even with different bits.

Chapter 11

Channels and Channel Capacity

Chapter 9 measured the source. This one measures the pipe, and produces the most quoted equation in communications. The binary symmetric channel and a Shannon–Hartley numerical have each appeared on three of the four papers, so this chapter is close to guaranteed marks.

11.1 The channel matrix

P(yj | xi) = probability of receiving yj given that xi was sent row rule : SUM over j of P(yj | xi) = 1 for every row i

Each row is one transmitted symbol, and reading across it tells you everything that symbol could turn into at the far end. Something must come out — the symbol cannot vanish — so every row sums to exactly 1.

The commonest mistake with channel matrices

Columns do not have to sum to 1. Only rows do. A column is all the ways one particular output could have arisen, and there is no law saying those add to anything in particular.

Your maximum error-free speed depends on only two things: how much frequency room you have, and how loud you are compared with the noise. Note the asymmetry — bandwidth sits outside the logarithm and power sits inside. Doubling B doubles the capacity; doubling S adds only a little. That is why 5G went hunting for millimetre-wave spectrum instead of just shouting louder.

The derivation in four lines

1. The received signal has power S + N, so its RMS voltage is √(S+N), and the noise alone is √N. You can only tell apart levels further apart than the noise, so the number of distinguishable levels is M = √(1 + S/N). 2. A pulse with M levels carries log2M = ½ log2(1 + S/N) bits. 3. Nyquist says a bandwidth B carries at most 2B pulses per second. 4. Multiply: C = B log2(1 + S/N).

11.4 Putting R and C together

Shannon’s channel coding theorem. If the information rate of a source does not exceed the capacity of the channel, then a coding technique exists that makes transmission possible through that unreliable channel with an arbitrarily low error rate.

Why that result is astonishing

Before 1948 every engineer believed that cutting errors meant slowing towards zero speed. Shannon showed you can run at full speed right up to C and still be near-perfect. You name the error rate; a code exists that beats it. The catch is that he proved such codes exist without saying how to build them — finding them took the next fifty years.

And the hard flip side: if R > C, reliable communication is impossible no matter how clever the coding. The trade-off that follows is the bandwidth-against-power one: wide bandwidth and low power (spread spectrum, GPS, whose signal arrives weaker than the noise and still works) or narrow bandwidth and high power (a broadcast tower). Both hit the same C.

Chapter 12

Carrier Modulation — ASK, FSK, PSK

Everything so far has been baseband. To go through the air the bits must ride on a carrier, and a sine wave has exactly three things you can change. That is why the list of schemes is so short. The 2025 paper asked for the three waveforms and the BPSK block diagrams as separate questions, which is five marks sitting in one chapter.

12.1 Why a carrier at all

  1. Antenna size. An efficient antenna is about a quarter of a wavelength long. At 1 kHz that is 75 km. At 1 GHz it is 7.5 cm. Raising the frequency is what makes radiating possible at all.
  2. Sharing the spectrum. If everybody transmitted at baseband, every signal would sit on top of every other. Different carriers separate them — that is frequency division multiplexing.
  3. The channel. Many channels will not pass low frequencies. Anything with a transformer or a capacitor in it blocks DC, and a baseband signal is full of it.

The carrier is a plain sine, A cos(2πfct + φ), and it carries no information on its own. Change A and you have ASK; change fc and you have FSK; change φ and you have PSK. There is no fourth option.

The one-line answer to “which is best”

BPSK, because for the same bandwidth as ASK it needs 3 dB less power for the same bit error rate, and unlike ASK it has a constant envelope so the transmitter amplifier can run in saturation. Its cost is that the receiver must recover the carrier coherently.

Why anyone would use DPSK instead

The whole subject in one screen

Everything, condensed

01The Whole Link, in One Diagram
  • The goal of a digital communication system: send information reliably and efficiently across a noisy channel.
  • The block diagram runs source → A/D → source encoder → channel encoder → modulator → channel → and back the same way in reverse.
  • Every receiver block exactly undoes the matching transmitter block — that mirror symmetry is the point of the diagram.
  • Source coding removes accidental redundancy; channel coding adds designed redundancy. Not a contradiction — different redundancy.
  • Scores: BER, SER, and SNRdB = 10 log10(Eb/N0). AWGN = additive, white, Gaussian noise, the standard channel model.
02Sampling and Quantisation
  • Nyquist: fs ≥ 2fm. Sample slower and a fast wave masquerades as a slow one — aliasing, and it cannot be undone.
  • Quantising: L = 2n levels, step = Vpp/2n, worst error = step/2, noise power Pq = step²/12.
  • SQNR for a full-scale sine is 6.02n + 1.76 dB; when Vrms is given, use Vrms² divided by step²/12 instead.
  • Every extra bit of the codeword buys about 6 dB of signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio.
  • n from a channel rate limit rounds down; n from an accuracy requirement rounds up.
03PCM Rates, Companding and Delta Modulation
  • Rb = n fs for one channel, Rb = N fs n for N channels on TDM; BT ≈ Rb/2 for NRZ and Rb for RZ.
  • A uniform quantiser is unfair to quiet signals: SQNR falls one dB for every dB the signal drops below full scale.
  • Companding compresses before quantising and expands after, giving fine steps near zero — mu-law (mu = 255) or A-law (A = 87.6).
  • 8-bit companded PCM matches roughly 12- to 13-bit uniform PCM on speech; that is the 64 kb/s telephone channel.
  • Delta modulation sends one bit per sample. Step × fs ≥ 2πfmA or you get slope overload; too large a step gives granular noise.
04Line Codes — the Six You Must Draw
  • Line codes exist to stop four faults: baseline wandering, loss of synchronisation, DC content, and no self-clocking.
  • NRZ holds the level all bit; RZ returns to zero mid-bit; unipolar uses one polarity, polar two, bipolar three levels.
  • AMI sends 0 as nothing and alternates the polarity of successive 1s — pencil the polarities before drawing.
  • Manchester puts a transition in the middle of every bit, so it is fully self-synchronising, at double the bandwidth.
  • AMI detects errors free, through bipolar violations; Manchester recovers the clock. Those are the two one-word answers.
05Baud, Bandwidth and Spectra
  • Data rate N is in bits/s; signal rate S is in baud. S = cN(1/r), and c = ½ for the average case.
  • Bit rate = pulse rate × log2L, so bit rate is never below pulse rate and they are equal only for binary signalling.
  • Nyquist maximum data rate: N_max = 2B log2L, and the minimum bandwidth equals the signal rate, B_min = S.
  • PSD: NRZ peaks at DC, AMI is zero at DC and humps near fT = 0.5, Manchester is zero at DC but runs about twice as wide.
  • AMI sends zeros as silence, so long zero runs break the clock — scramblers (B8ZS, HDB3) inject deliberate bipolar violations to fix it.
06ISI and the Nyquist Criteria
  • ISI is one symbol’s tail leaking into its neighbours’ sampling instants, caused by the channel being bandwidth limited — it is not noise, so more power does not help.
  • Nyquist’s first criterion: let the pulses overlap, but make p(nT) = 1 at n = 0 and 0 at every other sampling instant.
  • In frequency the same condition is the folding test — shifted copies of P(f) spaced 1/T apart must add up to a flat constant.
  • The minimum possible bandwidth is B_min = Rb/2, reached only by the unbuildable brick-wall sinc pulse.
  • Raised cosine: BT = (Rb/2)(1 + α), efficiency 2/(1 + α), with α = 0.2 to 0.5 in practice.
07The Eye, the Equaliser and Duobinary
  • The eye diagram overlays one-symbol slices of the received wave; a wide-open eye is a healthy link, a closed eye means ISI alone causes errors.
  • Read six things off an eye: optimum sampling instant, noise margin, slope, zero-crossing jitter, trace thickening, and the error-free sampling width.
  • A zero-forcing equaliser is a transversal filter whose taps force the output to zero at the N sampling instants either side — at the cost of amplifying noise.
  • Duobinary adds controlled ISI: precode by running XOR, map to ±1, then send y(k) = a(k) + a(k−1), giving three levels +2, 0, −2.
  • Duobinary decoding is memoryless — y = 0 means 1, y = ±2 means 0 — and it hits 2 bit/s/Hz with a buildable filter.
08Noise, the Matched Filter and Error Probability
  • S/N = (Eb/N0)(Rb/B). Eb/N0 is the fair comparison because it does not depend on receiver bandwidth.
  • The matched filter has h(t) = s(T − t) and gives the largest possible output SNR at t = T.
  • That maximum is 2E/N0 — it depends only on the pulse energy, never on its shape, and a correlator does exactly the same job.
  • Pe = Q(√(2Eb/N0)) for BPSK but Q(√(Eb/N0)) for ASK and coherent FSK — a 3 dB gap in BPSK’s favour.
  • Noise from a density: N = N0B, or in dBm add 10 log10 B to N0; the thermal floor at 290 K is −174 dBm/Hz.
09Information and Entropy
  • Information depends only on probability: I(x) = −log2P(x) bits. Rarer events carry more.
  • The logarithm is forced by the axiom that independent news must add, I(p1p2) = I(p1) + I(p2).
  • Entropy H(X) = −∑P log2P is the weighted average information per symbol, bounded by 0 ≤ H ≤ log2m.
  • H is maximum when all symbols are equally likely and zero when one symbol is certain; a fair coin gives exactly 1 bit.
  • Information rate R = r H(X) bits per second. Keep R (how fast the source talks) apart from C (how fast the channel listens).

  • The BPSK receiver must include carrier recovery — without it the link is not coherent, and a 180° lock error inverts every bit.
  • BPSK beats ASK and FSK by 3 dB, because its symbols sit 2A apart instead of A apart: Pe = Q(√(2Eb/N0)).
